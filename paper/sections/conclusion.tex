\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Relative to the methods summarised in Section~\ref{sec:related}, which classify one downsampled view of the photo and often
leave the split order unstated \cite{xu2024}, this work treated resolution as a variable and the evaluation protocol as part
of the method. A frozen DINOv3 encoder \cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, high-detail tiles at a common leaf scale and a small
attention head \cite{ilse2018} trained from photo labels gave a rice leaf disease classifier with a heat map. Under a
protocol that groups near-duplicates and capture sessions, it was clearly better than whole-photo use of the same encoder and
than simpler pooling, and not clearly different from a ConvNeXt-T fine-tuned at a larger input size; averaging the two kinds of
model helped further.

For a practitioner the lessons are these: keep lesion-scale detail, by tiles or by a larger input; split by capture session
before augmenting, and run shortcut controls; and do not read in-domain scores on plain backgrounds as field performance. The
open problem is transfer to field photos, where no method tested here held up, and the next steps are the encoder adaptation,
open-set handling and on-device work listed in Table~\ref{tab:limits}.
